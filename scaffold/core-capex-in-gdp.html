Where core capital goods orders sit in the economy

Exploration, 2026-11-12. Figures are from FRED (BEA national accounts, Census M3, BLS producer prices) and Pharos's SEC Company Facts, pulled for this page. Dollar levels are 2026 Q3 at annual rates unless marked.

  • Equipment investment is 6% of GDP, but on average it adds about 12% of GDP growth, and it drove about half of the 2008-09 contraction.
  • Core capital goods orders are the monthly, early read on the US-made part of that equipment. They move with equipment investment in the same quarter and one quarter ahead; they do not forecast further out.
  • Right now the equipment boom is computers, mostly imported, and four companies' spending accounts for about two-thirds of the rise in public-company capital spending. That is why real core orders (+9.3%) understate real equipment investment (+14.1%).

What core orders tell you that GDP does not

  • Timing, not foresight: orders track equipment investment in the same quarter and the next, then the link fades. Their value is that they arrive monthly, while GDP for a quarter arrives about a month after it ends and is revised for years.
  • Intent: an order is a commitment to spend, made before the goods exist. When businesses lose confidence, orders fall first; that is the lead you can see before each recession on the M3 chart.
  • What they miss: imports, software and R&D, buildings, and defense and aircraft. Right now imports are the big miss.

Decomposed into companies: the buyers

Census does not name who orders. The other end of the trade does: public companies report capital spending in their filings, and Pharos has it (SEC Company Facts, concept PaymentsToAcquirePropertyPlantAndEquipment, calendar years). Across the 3,450 companies reporting both years, capital spending rose from $903B in 2024 to $1,097B in 2025 (+22%).

The four highlighted companies went from $155.5B to $281.4B: $125.9B of the $195B rise, about two-thirds. Read with the table above, the boom is a computing build-out by a handful of buyers, filled largely with imported machines.

Not comparable dollar for dollar with GDP: company capital spending is worldwide, includes land and buildings, and is cash paid. Amazon does not appear: it reports under a different concept, so this concept misses it. Industry is the SEC's code for the company, not for what it bought.
